    Courses & Certificate
    Become a certified Trend Detective
    Three short lessons, five questions. Score 4 or more to earn your certificate.
    
    
    
      
    
        
    1 · What is a trend?
    A trend is change that keeps moving in one direction over time: rising, falling, thickening or thinning. Climate data also swings with the seasons and from year to year, so a trend is the slow signal hidden under that noise. Averaging months into yearly values, or removing the normal seasonal cycle, makes the signal easier to see.
    
        
    2 · What makes a trend significant?
    A significance test asks how often a pattern this strong would appear by luck if nothing were changing. That chance is the p-value. Scientists usually call a trend significant when p is below 0.05, meaning less than a 5% chance. Not significant does not mean no change: the record may be too short, or the change may hide in one season.
    
        
    3 · Mann-Kendall and Sen's slope
    The Mann-Kendall test compares every pair of years and counts how often the later year is higher. If ups clearly outnumber downs, the trend is unlikely to be chance. Sen's slope estimates the size of the change as the median of all pairwise slopes, so one extreme year cannot drag it. Both are standard tools in climate science because they do not assume the data follow a bell curve.
    
      
    
      
    
        
    
        
        
    
        
    
          
          
          
    
          
    
        
    
      
    
    
    
  
    

    



    
  
    
    
    About NE-AR
    Naturalists Explorer – Augmented Reality
    NE-AR began at the NASA Space Apps Challenge 2025, where it was named a Global Nominee. In 2026 we rebuilt it around one question: is the change real? Our aim is to help students and citizens read climate data the way scientists do. NASA has observed Earth from space for more than 60 years; this site uses 45 years of NASA POWER data (1981–2025).
    
    
    
    
    
      
    Method
      
        
    1. Download monthly data from the NASA POWER API for every grid point inside each region (1981–2025).
      2. 
        
    2. Average the grid points into one regional series per variable.
      3. 
        
    3. Build yearly or seasonal averages, which removes the seasonal cycle.
      4. 
        
    4. Test for a monotonic trend with Mann-Kendall (with tie correction) and estimate its size with Sen's slope; or fit a least-squares line and test it with a t-test and a 95% confidence interval.
      5. 
        
    5. Define normal as the 1981–2010 mean for each calendar month, plus or minus two standard deviations.
    
      
    Data sources
      
        
    • NASA POWER: monthly meteorology at 0.5° × 0.625°, from the MERRA-2 reanalysis produced by NASA's Global Modeling and Assimilation Office.
      • 
        
    • Variables: air temperature (mean, minimum, maximum), precipitation, relative humidity, snow depth, snow cover and root-zone soil wetness.
      • 
        
    • Future: NASA NEX-GDDP-CMIP6 daily downscaled projections (0.25°), lower-emissions SSP2-4.5 and higher-emissions SSP5-8.5. We take each model's change from its own 1995–2014 baseline and add it to the NASA POWER baseline for the same years, so the future joins the measured past.
      • 
        
    • Coming next: GRACE and GRACE-FO water storage for the Himalaya.
      • 
        
    • All statistics are computed in your browser. Nothing is sent anywhere.
